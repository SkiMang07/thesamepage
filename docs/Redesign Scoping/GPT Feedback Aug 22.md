## Overall recommendation

Shift the product from an “information dashboard” toward a “manager’s action brief.”

A manager should open TSP and immediately understand:

1. What needs my attention?
2. Who should I talk to?
3. What should I do next?

Currently, Mission Control first emphasizes the product’s information architecture—People, Work, Foundation—while the actionable signals sit below it. The three large teal panels are visually dominant, even though they mainly duplicate the sidebar.

## UX designer perspective

The strongest issues are:

- The dashboard’s biggest elements are navigation, not decisions.
- Cards, gradients, labels, badges, links, and section headings compete at similar weights.
- Many labels use small uppercase text, making the product feel more like an administrative console.
- Wide screens spread related information too far apart.
- The person workspace presents too many equally important modules simultaneously.
- Plain “Loading…” screens feel unfinished next to the otherwise polished UI.
- Repeated blue KPI tiles undermine the intended meaning of blue as the Scribe/AI color.

I would change:

- Replace the three large dashboard zone cards with one “Needs your attention” section containing 3–5 prioritized actions.
- Move the area summaries into a smaller secondary strip or rely on the sidebar.
- Increase ordinary text and metadata by roughly one size step.
- Reserve uppercase labels for occasional eyebrows, not every card heading.
- Reduce decorative gradients. Use teal for the primary action and brand moments; let most information sit on quiet white surfaces.
- Replace repeated `Prep →` buttons with contextual language such as “Prep Beth’s 1:1.”
- Use skeletons shaped like the incoming page instead of a floating “Loading…” line.
- On the person page, make “Next conversation” the primary workspace and move Development, Assessment, Expectations, and history behind clearer tabs or progressive disclosure.

## SaaS marketer perspective

The app’s visual language currently communicates:

> “Here is a comprehensive system containing your management information.”

Your differentiating promise is closer to:

> “Here is what to pay attention to, and how to handle it.”

That difference matters. The current experience risks resembling a lighter performance-management or HR platform—the category you are intentionally avoiding.

Specific messaging changes:

- Replace “Your team, at a glance” with something more consequential, such as “Here’s what needs your attention this week.”
- Rename generic metrics into decisions where possible:
    - “8 due” → “8 conversations to prepare”
    - “0/5 goals on track” → “5 goals need a check-in”
    - “Settings not finished” → the exact missing setup action
- Make TSP’s judgment visible on Mission Control. The useful AI-generated interpretation currently appears deeper inside the product.
- Treat Scribe as a capability, not the visual co-star of every screen. Right now, its bright blue button competes with the page’s main task.

The product should visibly demonstrate “judgment, not just data” within the first viewport.

## Prospective-customer perspective

As a newly promoted manager, my likely reaction would be:

> “This looks capable, but where do I start?”

Ten sidebar destinations, three large categories, several metrics, and multiple work areas suggest meaningful setup and maintenance. That creates anxiety before value.

What would increase confidence:

- One obvious recommended action.
- More human language and fewer system labels.
- Clear explanations of why something matters.
- Exact setup requests instead of percentage coverage or “not finished.”
- Strong reassurance around private notes and sensitive employee information.
- A calmer person page that feels designed for preparing a conversation, not maintaining a personnel record.

The 1:1 page is currently the clearest expression of the product’s wedge. It is focused, human, and task-oriented. I would use that page’s simplicity as the model for the rest of the product.

## The highest-impact first pass

I would make these changes in order:

1. Rebuild Mission Control around a prioritized action queue.
2. Remove the large duplicate navigation panels from the dashboard’s primary position.
3. Simplify the person workspace around the next conversation.
4. Establish a calmer typography hierarchy with less uppercase microcopy.
5. Remove blue from non-AI KPI tiles and reduce gradient usage.
6. Add purposeful loading skeletons.
7. Group the sidebar into lightweight sections such as People, Work, and Workspace—without adding more visual decoration.

The tension that matters is that the marketer wants the brand to feel distinctive, while the customer needs calm and clarity. The answer is not less personality; it is concentrating the personality in a few memorable places—the logo, primary actions, and Scribe—rather than distributing color across every metric.

My suggested next move is a side-by-side Mission Control mockup using your real data: current version versus an action-first version. That is the smallest change that would test whether this diagnosis is correct before touching the full app.
