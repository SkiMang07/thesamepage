# Point of view

Layer 3 of the brand foundation. The argument The Same Page would make in public even
if it sold nothing, and the things it is against. Read before writing any headline,
any blog post, or any message pillar in `messaging.md`.

Written cold, before reading a line of the current site copy, per the contamination
audit in `gtm/research/audience-2026-08.md`.

**The current site copy is scaffolding, not a fixed point.** No shipped line
constrains this.

---

## The argument, in one paragraph

Managers keep notes on their people in a place their people can't read. Everyone
treats that as the responsible thing to do, and it's the reason the whole system
fails. A record you keep in secret feels like a file you're building on someone, so
you keep it badly, or you stop. Then the year ends and you reconstruct it from
memory, and the person hears your account of their work for the first time in the
room where it's being used to judge them. The secrecy isn't a safeguard around the
record. It's the defect in it. Anything I'd write down about someone's work, they
should be able to read.

**The line, in the reader's grammar:** *anything I'd write about how someone's doing,
they should be able to read.*

---

## The five beliefs

Each one is arguable. A reasonable manager could disagree with any of them, which is
the test that this is a point of view rather than a description.

### 1. Anything I'd write about someone's work, they should be able to read.

The default runs the other way. Notes about a person are treated as sensitive,
therefore private, therefore locked down, and the entire category competes on how
finely it can slice who's allowed to see what. We think the permission model is the
wrong argument. The person the record is about is the one reader who has an
unqualified right to it.

### 2. The secrecy is why the record rots.

This is the load-bearing claim and the one most likely to be wrong. The conventional
explanation for why managers stop keeping notes is that they're busy. Busyness is
real and it isn't sufficient. Managers describe the failure as a double bind rather
than a scheduling problem: *"It always feels like I'm either slacking on it or
building a creepy dossier on my own team."* Both halves of that sentence feel bad,
which is why neither one wins. A record the person can read removes one half of the
bind, and the habit gets a chance. A faster private notebook doesn't touch it.

### 3. Who can read it decides what gets written.

The only place managers criticise the incumbent tools at length is HR visibility into
1:1 notes, and the documented outcome is that they write less honestly: *"Keep your
notes as objective as possible - leave out commentary."* That looks like an argument
against sharing. It's an argument about audience.

**Writing for HR and writing for the person are opposite pressures.** HR is a jury,
so you write defensively, you strip the commentary, and what's left is a thin record
that helps nobody. The person it's about isn't a jury, they're the other party to the
conversation, so you write the way you'd say it to their face. That's a plainer
standard than privacy produces and a higher one. The record gets shorter and truer at
the same time.

This is why "shared" and "visible to HR" aren't two points on one dial. They're
different objects.

### 4. The version of me that's thinking clearly should be the one deciding.

Managers can't calibrate and say so plainly: *"It's really hard for me to even assess
what's reasonable or not."* With nothing to measure against, the standard gets
invented privately, revised after the fact, and said out loud for the first time in
the room where it's being applied. That's the unfairness, and a better-written review
doesn't fix it.

The same failure runs on a much shorter clock. I know how I want to manage. I lose it
at four o'clock on a bad Thursday, make a call I'd never have made on Sunday, and
notice weeks later when someone walks me back through it and I hear myself say I
wasn't being true to how I actually wanted to do this.

Both are the same failure with the same fix: **write it down when you're calm and
close to the thing, and have it in front of you when you're neither.** A standard set
before the year it's judging. A note written the day it happened instead of
reconstructed in November. Your own principles in the room at the moment you're
drifting from them. None of that is supplying judgment I don't have. It's handing me
back judgment I already made, at the moment I can't reach it.

### 5. The judgment stays mine, and that's exactly what makes the help useful.

What a new manager says they want is *"a more experienced manager to ask."* The books
stop working at the hard part because they can't know the part that's hard, which is
this person, this quarter, against what I already said I expected. Generic advice is
generic by construction.

So the model earns its place by knowing my situation, and it knows my situation only
because I put it there. Recall, retrieval, prep, an argument about a call I'm about
to make, a draft I rewrite: welcome, and we'll say so plainly rather than hiding it.

**The boundary is saving, not drafting**, and the distinction carries real weight. A
model applying my own scale to evidence I wrote isn't judging anyone. It's the
clearest instance of belief 4 in the whole product: my standard, my notes, handed
back to me at the moment I'd otherwise be starting from a blank scorecard. What it
never does is save a value I didn't set, score from activity, or fill a gap the
evidence doesn't support. Undisclosed: worse than doing none of it. The manager who
owns the judgment is the only one who can be held to it, and being held to it is the
point.

## What we're against, named

A point of view that only has friends isn't one.

| The enemy | What it is | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **The dossier** | Any record about a person that the person can't read. Their word, twice, unprompted. | It's the thing managers already feel bad about, and the guilt is what kills the habit. |
| **HR owning the manager's notes** | A tool bought by People Ops where the manager's 1:1 notes are visible upward. | Well evidenced: it makes managers write worse notes, so the tool degrades the record it exists to hold. |
| **The retroactive standard** | The bar that only exists after the fact, in one person's head. | It turns a judgment into a verdict, and it's why reviews land as a surprise. |
| **Management advice as content** | Books, courses, posts, a chatbot with no memory of you. Advice that can't know the situation. | It misdiagnoses the problem as character (be more self-aware) and misprescribes the fix as more reading. Both put the fix out of reach. |
| **Automated judgment** | Ratings produced or nudged by a model, scores derived from activity. | It launders a decision nobody will own, in the one place a person deserves an owner. |

The list contains no product names. Lattice is what their company bought. What we're
against is the assumption underneath it.

## What we concede

Every one of these is a real cost, and saying them out loud is what makes the rest
credible.

**Two records, honestly named, is the true answer, and we haven't built the second
one.** Comp, HR risk, and what I say to my own manager about someone don't belong in
a shared record and never will. Managers already run a second private container for
exactly this. A product claiming one record covers everything would be lying and the
reader would know inside a week. What we argue is that the *performance* record, the
one that becomes the review, has no business being one of the private ones.

Stated as a principle this holds. Stated as a description of the product it doesn't
yet: there's no per-person compensation anywhere in the schema and no HR-risk object.
The only private lane that exists is `dev_plan_manager_notes`, an append-only log on
a development plan. Copy may argue the principle. Copy may not describe the second
record as though it's there.

**Sharing changes what gets written, and that costs something.** The half-formed
worry, the thing you're not sure about yet, the sentence you'd want to sit on for a
month. Some of that stops getting written down. We think what survives is worth more
than what's lost, and it's a trade rather than a free win.

**The calm version of me isn't automatically right.** Sometimes the drift is
learning. A manager six weeks in writes down principles that turn out naive, and the
in-the-moment call is the wiser one. A coach that only ever pulls you back to what
you already said produces consistency, and consistency isn't the same thing as
getting better. So the mechanism has to include revising what you wrote, not only
reciting it: *here's what you said, here's what's different now, which one do you
mean?*

**This needs something in the cup.** It works because the manager has articulated
what they expect and how they want to manage. A first-time manager often hasn't, and
that's our ICP. Either the product helps them form those, or it runs thin until they
do. Pretending otherwise oversells the first week.

**A visible record doesn't fix the week you don't open the app.** *"The tool really
doesn't matter; but proper discipline to use it does."* That objection is correct and
copy can't move it. It's a product problem, and this point of view is writing a
cheque the product has to cash.

**The person reading the record isn't the one paying for it.** We argue for a second
audience who'll never be the buyer. A genuine business cost, taken deliberately,
because that audience is the mechanism.

## What would prove us wrong

Belief 2 is the falsifiable one and the whole structure rests on it.

- Managers with 4-11 reports who share the record and still let it decay at the same
  rate. That'd mean the guilt wasn't load-bearing and busyness was the whole story.
- Managers who share a record and keep a private one at the same volume anyway. That'd
  mean the shared record is theatre and the real one is still hidden.
- Reports who read the record and are worse off for it. The cardinal-insult risk runs
  through here: if being seen reads as being watched even when it's disclosed, the
  resolution doesn't hold.
- Managers who write down their principles and drift from them at exactly the same
  rate. That'd break belief 4 and take the coaching pillar with it.

None of these have been tested. The mechanism is evidenced by managers describing it.
It hasn't been observed in use.

## What this rules out for layers 4 and 5

- **No permission-granularity argument.** Fine-grained sharing controls are the
  category's argument and they concede our point.
- **No time-saved lead.** Already a standing rule; this gives it a reason. Our claim
  is that the record is true and already seen, and a free model can beat us on speed
  all day without touching that.
- **No "know your team better" framing.** One syllable from the thing they're afraid
  of being.
- **The coach never scolds.** A mentor saying *you weren't being true to yourself*
  lands because they know you and have earned the standing. The same sentence from
  software reads as being lectured by a form you filled out. Layer 5 inherits this:
  the register for recall is even and factual, never disappointed.
- **The bar metaphor stays on trial.** Belief 4 needs a name for the standard set in
  advance. "The bar" is ours, appears once in ~120 quotes, in the opposite direction.
  Layer 4 picks a name and defends it on merit. Test against: *what I expect*, *what
  the role asks for*, *what we agreed*.

## Open items

- **The product doesn't cash belief 1 yet.** The invite and claim mechanism is in the
  schema, `frontend/app/app/ic/page.tsx` is a 56-line stub, and the report-facing view
  isn't built. Honest as an argument, premature as a feature claim. Layer 4 must not
  put "your report can see it" under a pillar as shipped proof until it is.
- **No first-class place for the manager's own principles.** Belief 4 and 5 assume the
  manager has stated how they want to manage. Today that arrives as an uploaded
  document through the Context Engine and appears in Scribe only as an eval case
  ("person history combined with leadership principles"). There's no object called
  *how I manage*. That's a product gap the messaging depends on.
- **The anchoring guardrail on the assessment draft.** A drafted 3 pulls a manager who'd
  have said 2. Review is thinner protection than it sounds. The fix is to make the
  anchor auditable rather than to remove the draft: show the evidence beside each
  drafted score, and surface what the model deliberately left blank instead of
  omitting it silently. That turns the weakness into the demo.
- **Eval coverage on the assessment draft.** Third AI call site to cover, behind
  wrap-up extraction and `/prep`, both of which were flagged first.
- Belief 2 needs one round of interviews. It's the spine and it's an assertion.
- Naming the standard is a layer 4 decision and it's on the critical path.
